A ML technique that worked perfectly and did nothing

Covariance denoising cut our correlation matrix's condition number by 59%, exactly as the literature says it should. It changed the portfolio weights by precisely zero. The reason turned out to be worth more than the technique.

Portfolio Management project · MSc Financial Engineering, WorldQuant University · Written up by Jackline Jebet

We spent a while convinced we had a bug.

The setup was simple enough. Ten large-cap US stocks, two years of daily prices, a Kelly optimizer, and a mandate saying no single holding could exceed 20%. Then we applied covariance denoising, a standard piece of the quantitative finance toolkit. It strips statistical noise out of a correlation matrix before you hand that matrix to an optimizer.

It ran fine. Diagnostics looked healthy. The condition number dropped from 16.96 to 6.97, a 59% improvement, roughly what you would expect from the literature.

Then we compared the new portfolio weights against the old ones. They were the same. Not similar. The same, to every decimal place we printed.

We checked whether the wrong matrix had gone into the optimizer. It hadn't. We checked whether the denoising function was quietly returning its input unchanged. It wasn't; the matrix really had changed. The explanation, once we found it, was the most useful thing we got out of the whole project. And it isn't specific to portfolios.

Why nothing moved

The mandate capped any position at 20%. That one rule does more than it looks like it does. One divided by 0.20 is five, so at least five positions have to carry weight regardless of what the data says. Our optimizer held exactly five names, and every one sat at 20.0%.

Every active position was pressed against the edge of what the mandate allowed.

That is what killed the denoising. Normally an optimizer picks among interior solutions, weighing assets against each other on their estimated returns and how they move together. Improve those estimates and the weighing shifts. But our solution was not interior. It was jammed into a corner of the feasible region, and that corner is defined by the rule, not by the data. You can hand that optimizer a better covariance matrix all day. It has nowhere to go.

Nobody looks at a 20% position limit and thinks of it as a modelling decision. It reads like governance. A box you tick. But it was doing all the work, and the analysis was taking the credit.

Most people reading this have sat in a meeting where a disappointing result was met with a proposal to build a better model. Sometimes that is the right call. Sometimes the model was never the binding thing, and a mandate rule or a liquidity floor or a policy nobody has revisited in three years is quietly deciding the outcome. Checking which one binds costs nothing. Building the better model costs a quarter.

Sharpe ratios cannot see leverage

While we were in there, we compared the same portfolio at half, full and double leverage, borrowing at the risk-free rate to fund the extra exposure.

We checked that column too. Also not a bug.

Excess return scales linearly with leverage, so doubling exposure doubles both the average excess return and its standard deviation. The Sharpe ratio is one divided by the other. It cannot register the change, by construction.

Now look at what does change. Worst drawdown goes from 3.2% to 13.1%. Expected loss on a bad day roughly quadruples. Those are three very different things to live with, and the most quoted number in finance rates all of them identically.

There is a further wrinkle we only found by validating properly. Kelly's theory says growth stops improving past a certain amount of leverage: the drag from compounding losses starts to outweigh the benefit of compounding gains. In the weakest of our five validation periods, double leverage grew at 6.12% a year against full leverage's 7.38%, while carrying twice the drawdown. Worse return, more pain.

On the single test quarter, which happened to be a rising market, double leverage looked like the obvious answer. It won by a mile. Had we stopped there we would have drawn exactly the wrong conclusion.

Equal weight was hard to beat

We tested six portfolios across five validation periods, purging data around each test window so nothing from the future leaked backwards into training. This matters more than people assume. Shuffle a return series the way you would shuffle an ordinary dataset and the results stop meaning anything.

On the single out-of-sample quarter, the best combination — hierarchical risk parity with denoising — won five of six metrics. Near enough a clean sweep.

Across all five periods it came apart.

A plain equal-weight portfolio, one-tenth in each stock, had the highest average growth rate of anything we tested. No estimation. No covariance matrix. No code beyond dividing by ten. DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal published this result in 2009 and it has been replicated many times, so it should not have been a surprise. There is a difference between reading a finding and watching it happen to your own work.

Look at the standard deviation column, though, because that is the real finding. HRP's mean cross-validated Sharpe was 2.32 with a standard deviation of 2.31. The dispersion equals the mean. In one of the five folds, four of the six portfolios posted negative Sharpe ratios. Against variation that size, a difference of 0.1 in mean Sharpe is not a result. It is noise with a decimal point.

One thing did survive validation: hierarchical risk parity reduced downside risk consistently, cutting mean drawdown from −10.59% to −7.49% and cVaR from −2.79% to −1.92%. That is the claim we are comfortable making, and it is a narrower one than the test quarter suggested.

What we would take from it

Diagnose before treating. Denoising did nothing here because our data barely had the problem it solves — 437 observations for 10 assets means the covariance was already well estimated. The technique is built for the opposite case, hundreds of assets in a short window. Applying it to our problem was effort without return, and knowing which regime you are in is more valuable than knowing the technique.

And the constraint matters more than the estimate when the constraint binds. The most useful question in the whole project cost nothing to ask: what is actually determining this answer?
