The formula works because nobody believes it

Black-Scholes assumes returns are normally distributed. Every single stock in my own research universe contradicts that, some of them badly. The interesting part is not that the assumption is wrong. It is that the market has known for decades and prices around it anyway.

Jackline Jebet · MSc Financial Engineering, WorldQuant University

While working on a portfolio project last month I printed the descriptive statistics for ten large US stocks, the way you do before touching anything else. Means, volatilities, skew, kurtosis. It is a housekeeping step and I nearly scrolled past it.

Then I looked at the kurtosis column properly.

A normal distribution has excess kurtosis of exactly zero. That is the benchmark. So the lowest reading here, Exxon at 2.44, is already a long way from normal, and Meta at 16.33 is in a different postcode entirely. Not one of the ten came close. This was 437 trading days of ordinary large-cap data, nothing exotic, no crisis window selected to make a point.

Which matters because the most widely used formula in finance assumes those numbers should all be zero.

How the assumption got in

It arrived early, and for a good reason.

In 1900 a French mathematician named Louis Bachelier submitted a thesis at the Sorbonne called Théorie de la spéculation, in which he modelled stock prices as Brownian motion. Brownian motion is the random jitter of particles suspended in fluid, first described by the botanist Robert Brown in 1827 while watching pollen grains under a microscope. Bachelier's idea was that prices wander in the same undirected way, which was a genuinely radical claim about markets at the time and was largely ignored for fifty years.

It also had a problem. If prices themselves follow Brownian motion, they can wander below zero, and a share price of negative four dollars is not a thing that happens.

Paul Samuelson came across the work in the mid 1950s and fixed it with a modification that looks small and is not. Instead of prices following Brownian motion, let the logarithm of prices follow it. That change guarantees prices stay positive, and it gives you geometric Brownian motion, which is the engine underneath Black-Scholes and most of what came after.

However, taking logs carries a consequence that rarely gets stated as plainly as it should. If log prices follow Brownian motion, then log returns are normally distributed. That is not an optional extra. It is the same assumption viewed from a different angle, and it is what makes the mathematics tractable. Without it there is no closed-form solution, no tidy partial differential equation, no formula you can put on a calculator.

The normality assumption is not a simplification bolted onto the model. It is the thing that makes the model solvable at all.

Fischer Black spent the back half of the 1960s working on exactly this problem, coming at it through warrants rather than options because warrants traded more actively then. He had the differential equation by 1969 and could not solve it. Myron Scholes joined him, Robert Merton derived the result independently and initially thought the approach was wrong, and the paper appeared in 1973. Within a few years the Wall Street Journal was running advertisements for calculators with the formula built in. Scholes and Merton took the Nobel in 1997, by which time Black had died.

What none of them could do, and what nobody has done since, is remove the assumption while keeping the answer.

What fat tails actually cost you

Excess kurtosis of 16 sounds abstract. In practice it means large moves happen far more often than the normal distribution allows, and the gap is not small. Under normality, moves of five or six standard deviations are supposed to be so rare that you would not expect to see one in several human lifetimes of trading. In real equity data they turn up every few years, and in 2008 several arrived in the same week.

So a model built on normality will systematically underprice the things that hurt most. An option that pays out only in a large move is worth more than the formula says, because large moves are more common than the formula assumes. Anyone selling those options at the model price is selling insurance too cheaply, and the bill does not arrive gradually.

This is not a theoretical concern, and it shows up in my own work in a second way. Because I was building a Kelly-optimal portfolio, which maximises expected log growth, the tails mattered more than usual. Log utility is severely punished by large losses, so estimating the distribution with a Gaussian approximation would have thrown away exactly the behaviour the objective function cares about most. So we optimised directly on the empirical return distribution instead. Not out of theoretical purity, but because the kurtosis column told us the shortcut would have been lying to us.

The part I find genuinely interesting

None of this is news. Practitioners have known the assumption fails since at least the 1987 crash, which produced a single-day move that geometric Brownian motion says should essentially never occur. Before 1987, options across different strike prices traded at roughly similar implied volatilities, which is what you would expect if the market believed the model. After 1987, they did not, and they never have again.

That pattern has a name. Plot implied volatility against strike price and you get the volatility smile, or in equities more often a skew, with downside strikes priced at higher implied volatility than the model would imply. Which means the market is charging extra for exactly the tail events Black-Scholes says are negligible.

Nevertheless the formula is still everywhere, and this is the bit worth sitting with. It survives not because anyone believes its assumptions, but because it has become a translation layer. Quoting an option price in dollars tells you little. Quoting it as an implied volatility, which means the volatility you would have to plug into Black-Scholes to recover the observed market price, gives you a number you can compare across strikes, maturities and underlyings.

The model failed as a description of the world and then succeeded as a common language for disagreeing about it.

So when a trader quotes a strike at 24 vol, they are not asserting that returns are normally distributed. They are using a formula everyone agrees is wrong, in a way everyone understands, as a unit of measurement. The volatility surface is a running record of the ways the market thinks the model is wrong, expressed in the model's own vocabulary.

Worth being clear about

The ten-stock sample above is from a specific universe over 437 trading days. Fat tails in equity returns are a well-established finding and my numbers are consistent with a large literature, so I am not claiming a discovery, only that I checked rather than assumed.

There are also models that relax the normality assumption directly, including stochastic volatility approaches and jump-diffusion models. They fit observed prices better and they are used. However they cost tractability and add parameters that have to be estimated, which is its own source of error, and none of them has displaced Black-Scholes as the quoting convention.

Why I keep returning to this

Three of my projects have now ended in the same place. In a portfolio study, the position cap turned out to matter more than the covariance estimate we spent the effort improving. In a correlation study, the crisis everyone teaches as diversification failing showed the hedge working, while the year it actually broke gets far less attention. And here, a formula used trillions of times a year rests on an assumption that ten minutes with a kurtosis column contradicts.

The pattern is not that the models are bad. They are extraordinary pieces of work and I use them. It is that the assumptions underneath them are load-bearing, they are usually invisible, and checking them is nearly always cheaper than the alternative.

Fischer Black stared at an unsolvable equation for years. The least I can do is look at the summary statistics before I use his answer.
